# Senior and Staff Behavioral Interview Preparation

This module prepares Product AI Engineer, Forward Deployed Engineer, and ML/AI Platform Engineer candidates for behavioral and leadership interviews. Use it with the [preparation curriculum](README.md), the [Product AI guide](04-product-ai-engineer.md), the [FDE guide](05-forward-deployed-engineer.md), and the [ML/AI Platform guide](06-ml-ai-platform-engineer.md).

Behavioral interviews are evidence interviews. The interviewer is not asking whether you know the vocabulary of leadership. They are testing whether your past decisions show the scope, judgment, influence, accountability, and learning expected at the target level.

This guide does not define or infer any company's values. Obtain the actual competencies from the recruiter, job description, interview brief, and public company material. Adapt your evidence to those sources without changing the facts of your experience.

## How Senior and Staff Evidence Differ

Seniority is not measured by how dramatic a story sounds. It is measured by the difficulty and reach of the decisions you owned, the quality of your judgment, and the durable effect of your work.

### Scope

At senior level, you can independently own a substantial problem from unclear requirements through production results. The scope may span a feature, service, customer deployment, platform capability, or important migration.

At staff level, the evidence normally crosses a team boundary. You may set direction for a product area, align several teams, change an organizational interface, create a shared technical capability, or resolve a risk that no single team can own alone.

Do not convert team size into a level claim. A small project can require staff judgment if it changes a high-consequence organizational decision. A large project can still show only local execution if you followed a plan set by others.

### Ambiguity

A senior candidate turns an unclear problem into goals, constraints, owners, milestones, and evidence. They identify what must be learned before committing.

A staff candidate also decides which ambiguity matters across a broader system. They frame the problem for other teams, expose conflicts between local goals, and sequence decisions so the organization can learn without making an irreversible bet too early.

### Influence

A senior candidate aligns immediate partners through clear reasoning, trust, prototypes, data, and direct conversation.

A staff candidate influences people who do not report to them and may have different incentives. They build a decision process, make disagreement productive, preserve dissent, and create enough shared context that teams can act consistently after the meeting ends.

Influence is not winning every argument. Strong evidence includes changing your own position when another person has better evidence.

### Leverage

A senior candidate delivers an important result and leaves the system easier to operate.

A staff candidate increases the effectiveness of many engineers or teams. Common forms of leverage include:

- A stable platform interface or paved road
- A reusable evaluation method
- A migration pattern
- A decision framework
- A safety or privacy control
- An operating mechanism such as an incident review or launch gate
- Technical mentoring that changes how others make decisions

The artifact alone is not leverage. Show adoption, reduced repeated work, safer decisions, faster delivery, or another observable downstream effect.

### Judgment

Senior and staff answers must explain why a choice fit the context. Name credible alternatives, constraints, risks, reversibility, and the evidence available at the time.

Staff-level judgment often includes portfolio choices:

- Which problem should the organization solve first?
- Which capability should be centralized?
- What should remain team-owned?
- When should a prototype be discarded, hardened, or stopped?
- Which risk requires a policy, and which requires local judgment?
- When is alignment worth waiting for, and when must a decision be made?

Avoid hindsight theater. Explain what was knowable then, not only what became obvious later.

### Accountability

Accountability means owning the quality of the decision and the response to its consequences. It does not mean claiming every task or accepting blame for failures outside your control.

Strong answers identify:

- The decision you owned
- The commitments you made
- What you delegated and how you maintained visibility
- What you missed
- How you communicated risk or bad news
- What you did after the result became clear
- What operating or technical change followed

Use `I` for your actions and `we` for shared outcomes. Naming another person's contribution accurately increases credibility.

### Organizational impact

Senior impact often appears in product outcomes, customer outcomes, reliability, delivery speed, quality, cost, or team capability.

Staff impact should connect technical direction to a broader organizational outcome. Examples include:

- Several teams adopted a common evaluation contract.
- A migration removed a recurring reliability risk.
- Field evidence changed the product roadmap.
- A platform reduced duplicate infrastructure and clarified ownership.
- A safety review prevented a risky launch and produced a reusable launch standard.
- Mentoring created new technical owners and reduced a bottleneck around you.

Organizational impact does not require a fabricated percentage. Directly observable evidence can be strong: a decision was adopted, a launch was stopped, ownership moved, support escalations declined, or teams retired duplicate systems.

### Calibrate the same story by level

For senior evidence, emphasize:

- Independent ownership
- Technical and product depth
- Ambiguity resolved
- Cross-functional execution
- Production consequences
- Measured result and learning

For staff evidence, add:

- Why the problem mattered beyond one team
- Which organizational boundary created friction
- How you aligned different incentives
- What strategy or mechanism outlasted your direct involvement
- How adoption, migration, or governance worked
- How you created other owners

Do not inflate a senior story into a staff story. If the effect stopped when you stopped pushing, it may show strong execution but limited durable leverage.

## Build Answers with SAIL and STAR

SAIL and STAR solve different preparation problems. SAIL helps you choose and shape evidence around seniority. STAR helps an interviewer follow the event.

### SAIL

Use SAIL to test whether a story contains enough leadership evidence:

1. Scope - What problem, boundary, duration, stakes, and people were involved? What did you own?
2. Actions - What did you personally decide, build, communicate, change, or stop?
3. Impact - What changed for users, customers, teams, systems, risk, cost, or strategy?
4. Learning - What did you learn, what changed afterward, and what would you do differently?

SAIL is a filter, not a script. If one element is weak, improve the evidence or choose another story.

### STAR

Use STAR to deliver the story:

1. Situation - Give only the context needed to understand the stakes.
2. Task - State the outcome you owned, the constraint, and why it was difficult.
3. Action - Explain your decisions and individual actions in sequence.
4. Result - State the outcome, evidence, trade-offs, and learning.

The action section should take most of the answer. Interviewers can read context on a resume. They need to know how you operated.

### Combine them

Prepare with SAIL, deliver with STAR:

- SAIL Scope becomes STAR Situation and Task.
- SAIL Actions becomes STAR Action.
- SAIL Impact and Learning become STAR Result.

Before using a story, ask:

- Can I state my decision in one sentence?
- Can I name at least two concrete actions that I took?
- Can I explain a credible alternative and why I rejected it?
- Can I show an outcome without inventing precision?
- Can I name a mistake, limitation, or new understanding?
- Can I answer what another person disagreed with?

### Make individual actions concrete

Weak action language hides evidence:

- "We aligned stakeholders."
- "We improved the model."
- "We fixed the incident."
- "I drove the project."

Replace it with observable behavior:

- "I wrote a one-page decision memo comparing a managed API with self-hosting across privacy, latency, cost, and operational burden."
- "I split evaluation failures into retrieval, instruction-following, tool-use, and policy categories, then assigned owners to the two dominant classes."
- "I paused the rollout, established a single incident channel, and asked the serving owner to cap concurrency while I verified whether queued requests could be replayed safely."
- "I met the security lead before the review, incorporated the threat model into the launch plan, and removed raw prompt logging from the proposed design."

### Quantify without fabrication

Use the strongest evidence your records support:

1. Exact measured result - use a number when you know its definition, source, time window, and baseline.
2. Defensible range - use a range when measurement was approximate and explain why.
3. Count or milestone - state customers launched, teams migrated, services retired, incidents avoided, or owners trained.
4. Before-and-after behavior - explain a concrete workflow or operational change.
5. Decision evidence - state what leadership, customers, or teams decided because of the work.
6. Honest uncertainty - say what was not measured and what proxy you used.

Never reverse-engineer a number because it sounds impressive. If you do not remember an exact value, say, "I do not remember the exact figure; the measure we used was weekly successful task completion, and it improved enough to pass our launch gate." Be ready to define the gate.

Separate outputs from outcomes:

- Output - shipped an evaluation service.
- Adoption - six teams integrated it.
- Operational outcome - teams caught regressions before deployment.
- Organizational impact - release reviews used one shared quality contract.

### Explain trade-offs

A senior answer should name:

- The options you considered
- The constraints that mattered
- The risk of your chosen option
- Why the decision was reversible or justified
- What evidence would have changed your mind

A staff answer should also explain:

- Who benefited and who absorbed the cost
- Whether the choice created a precedent
- How local optimization affected other teams
- How the organization would revisit the decision

### Show learning without self-destruction

Learning is not a ceremonial sentence at the end. It should connect the event to a changed behavior, design, or mechanism.

Use this structure:

1. "My mistake was..."
2. "I recognized it when..."
3. "I corrected it by..."
4. "Afterward, I changed..."
5. "In a later situation, that change helped..."

Choose a real mistake with meaningful consequences. Do not choose an ethical breach, blame others, or turn the answer into a disguised strength.

## Build a 12-Story Bank

Create one primary story for each slot. A single event may support more than one competency, but your bank should contain enough distinct events that the interview loop does not hear the same story repeatedly.

### 1. Major impact

Choose a project where your decisions materially changed a user, customer, platform, business, or organizational outcome.

Worksheet:

- Situation - What was the baseline and why did it matter?
- Ownership - Which outcome and decisions were yours?
- Scale - Which users, customers, services, or teams were affected?
- Actions - What three actions best explain the result?
- Trade-off - What valuable alternative did you decline?
- Impact - What measured or observable change occurred?
- Leverage - What continued after your direct work?
- Learning - What would you now do earlier?

Prompt: "Tell me about your most important impact in the last few years."

### 2. Failure

Choose a consequential failure where you had real agency. The story should demonstrate diagnosis, accountability, recovery, and changed behavior.

Worksheet:

- Expected outcome - What did you believe would happen?
- Miss - Which assumption, decision, or execution gap was yours?
- Signal - When did you realize the plan was failing?
- Response - What did you communicate, stop, repair, or re-scope?
- Consequence - Who was affected and how?
- Learning - What changed in your method or the system?
- Recurrence - What evidence shows the learning persisted?

Prompt: "Tell me about a failure that changed how you work."

### 3. Conflict

Choose a disagreement with a capable peer, manager, customer, product leader, safety reviewer, or infrastructure partner. Avoid stories where the other person was plainly unreasonable.

Worksheet:

- Decision - What specific decision was contested?
- Positions - What did each person believe, and why?
- Incentives - What legitimate concern sat behind each position?
- Actions - How did you listen, test evidence, and reduce heat?
- Movement - What changed in your view and theirs?
- Outcome - How was the decision made?
- Relationship - What happened to trust afterward?

Prompt: "Tell me about a difficult technical or product disagreement."

### 4. Influence without authority

Choose a story where the required people did not report to you and had competing priorities.

Worksheet:

- Goal - What could not be achieved by your team alone?
- Map - Who had decision power, expertise, workload, or veto power?
- Resistance - Why was the proposal not already happening?
- Influence - Which evidence, prototype, memo, coalition, or conversation changed the path?
- Concession - What did you change to earn alignment?
- Outcome - What did others choose to do?
- Durability - Did the behavior continue without your presence?

Prompt: "Tell me about a change you led without formal authority."

### 5. Ambiguous project

Choose a project that began with unclear users, requirements, feasibility, ownership, or success criteria.

Worksheet:

- Request - What vague request did you receive?
- Unknowns - Which unknown could invalidate the project?
- Discovery - Whom did you interview and what evidence did you gather?
- Frame - How did you define user, outcome, baseline, constraints, and non-goals?
- Sequence - What reversible step reduced uncertainty first?
- Decision - What did you build, defer, or stop?
- Outcome - What became clear because of your approach?

Prompt: "Tell me about a project where the problem was poorly defined."

### 6. Architecture decision

Choose a decision with credible alternatives and long-term consequences. The story should connect architecture to users, operations, cost, risk, and migration.

Worksheet:

- Context - What system and constraint created the decision?
- Options - Which two or three alternatives were credible?
- Criteria - How did you compare them?
- Dissent - Who disagreed, and what did they see?
- Decision - What did you choose and what risk did you accept?
- Rollout - How did you test, migrate, observe, and roll back?
- Result - Did the assumptions hold?
- Revisit - What would trigger a new decision?

Prompt: "Tell me about an architectural decision that had broad consequences."

### 7. Customer escalation

Choose a situation with urgency, trust risk, conflicting expectations, or commercial pressure. The customer can be external or an important internal platform user.

Worksheet:

- Impact - What was the customer unable to do?
- Stakes - What deadline, trust, safety, or business concern existed?
- Ownership - What did you own versus account, product, or support partners?
- Communication - What did you say before the answer was known?
- Diagnosis - How did you separate symptom, root cause, and requested solution?
- Trade-off - What immediate relief and durable fix did you choose?
- Outcome - What happened to the customer and relationship?
- Feedback - What product or operating change followed?

Prompt: "Tell me about a serious customer escalation you handled."

### 8. Incident

Choose a production event that tests judgment under incomplete information. It may involve model behavior, data, serving, security, or a dependency.

Worksheet:

- Detection - How was the incident found?
- Impact - Which users or workloads were affected?
- Role - Were you incident lead, technical lead, communicator, or service owner?
- First decisions - What did you protect, stop, or degrade?
- Coordination - How did you assign work and maintain a shared picture?
- Recovery - How did you validate safe restoration?
- Root causes - Which technical and organizational conditions contributed?
- Follow-through - What prevention, detection, or response capability changed?

Prompt: "Tell me about a production incident where you had to make decisions quickly."

### 9. Prioritization

Choose a constrained period where several valuable projects competed for time, people, compute, or organizational attention.

Worksheet:

- Choices - Which work could not all be done?
- Criteria - How did you compare value, urgency, confidence, risk, effort, and reversibility?
- Input - Whose context changed the ranking?
- Decision - What did you fund, defer, narrow, or stop?
- Communication - How did you explain the decision to the losing side?
- Outcome - What happened, including the cost of the choice?
- Reassessment - Which signal would have changed the priority?

Prompt: "Tell me about a hard prioritization decision."

### 10. Mentoring

Choose a story where another person became more capable or independent. Avoid making their growth about your brilliance.

Worksheet:

- Need - What capability or ownership gap existed?
- Consent - What did the person want to develop?
- Diagnosis - What behavior or skill was holding them back?
- Support - What feedback, opportunities, context, or guardrails did you provide?
- Ownership - What did you stop doing so they could lead?
- Outcome - What could they do independently afterward?
- Learning - How did you adapt your mentoring style?

Prompt: "Tell me about someone whose growth you supported."

### 11. Strategy

Choose a story where you shaped direction beyond one delivery milestone.

Worksheet:

- Landscape - What user, customer, technical, or organizational change mattered?
- Problem - Which repeated constraint deserved strategic attention?
- Alternatives - What could the organization invest in instead?
- Thesis - What direction did you propose and what assumptions supported it?
- Alignment - How did you incorporate product, engineering, safety, finance, or customer perspectives?
- Sequence - What was the first reversible commitment?
- Adoption - How did the strategy become funded or practiced?
- Revision - What evidence changed the roadmap?

Prompt: "Tell me about a technical strategy you helped create."

### 12. Ethical or safety decision

Choose a real tension involving privacy, unsafe behavior, fairness, user deception, excessive automation, data rights, security, or harmful misuse. Do not imply that one person resolved a broad ethical question alone.

Worksheet:

- Risk - Who could be harmed and through what failure path?
- Evidence - What was observed, uncertain, or missing?
- Pressure - What schedule, revenue, customer, or competitive force favored proceeding?
- Responsibility - What decision or escalation was yours?
- Action - What guardrail, review, scope reduction, disclosure, or stop decision did you pursue?
- Dissent - Who disagreed and why?
- Outcome - What launched, changed, or did not launch?
- Mechanism - What repeatable policy, review, or monitoring followed?

Prompt: "Tell me about a time you pushed back on an AI system because of safety, privacy, or ethical risk."

## Deliver the Story in 30, 90, or 180 Seconds

Prepare all three lengths. Do not compress by speaking faster. Remove lower-value context and preserve the decision.

### 30-second version

Use for an opening summary or when an interviewer asks for a quick example.

1. 5 seconds - situation and stakes
2. 5 seconds - your ownership
3. 15 seconds - decisive action and trade-off
4. 5 seconds - outcome and learning

Template:

"Our team needed to [outcome] while [constraint]. I owned [decision or result]. I [decisive action], choosing [option] over [alternative] because [reason]. The result was [measured or observable outcome], and I changed [later behavior or mechanism]."

### 90-second version

Use as the default first answer.

1. 15 seconds - situation
2. 10 seconds - task and personal ownership
3. 45 seconds - two or three actions, including conflict or trade-off
4. 20 seconds - result, limitation, and learning

At 90 seconds, stop and let the interviewer choose the follow-up. A complete opening should create useful doors without answering every possible question.

### 180-second version

Use when the interviewer explicitly requests depth or gives uninterrupted time.

1. 25 seconds - context, baseline, and stakes
2. 20 seconds - ownership, constraints, and success criteria
3. 90 seconds - actions in decision order
4. 25 seconds - result, measurement, and downstream effect
5. 20 seconds - learning and what you would change

The 180-second version should add reasoning, not extra chronology. Include the alternative, dissent, uncertainty, and validation.

### Compression drill

1. Record the 180-second answer.
2. Transcribe it.
3. Mark each sentence as context, decision, action, impact, learning, or filler.
4. Remove repeated context and generic claims.
5. Record the 90-second version.
6. Reduce it to a 30-second index without losing ownership or outcome.
7. Ask a partner to state your decision, action, and impact from memory.

If the partner remembers only the technology or team context, the story needs a clearer decision.

## AI-Specific Behavioral Topics

AI work adds uncertainty that cannot be hidden behind a single metric. Strong answers show how you combined model evidence, user outcomes, system constraints, risk, and human judgment.

### Uncertain model quality

Show that you did not claim certainty from a small benchmark.

Cover:

- The task and consequences of a wrong answer
- Failure taxonomy rather than only an average score
- Slice analysis for important users or workflows
- Human review and disagreement handling
- Offline and online evidence
- Confidence limits, unknowns, and launch guardrails
- Fallback, abstention, or human escalation
- Monitoring after distribution shift

Useful tension: the model showed promising aggregate quality, but a high-stakes slice remained weak.

### Evaluation disagreement

Evaluation disagreements often hide different definitions of success.

Explain:

- Whether the disagreement concerned labels, rubric, dataset, grader, threshold, or business objective
- Who represented user, domain, model, product, and risk perspectives
- How examples were sampled
- How ambiguous cases were adjudicated
- Which metric or slice became the decision gate
- What remained unresolved
- How the evaluation contract was versioned and revisited

Do not say, "The data proved I was right," without explaining who selected the data and what it omitted.

### Safety or privacy pushback

Strong pushback protects the underlying goal while challenging the unsafe path.

Explain:

- The concrete harm or exposure
- Data flow, retention, access, or model behavior at issue
- The evidence and uncertainty
- The stakeholder pressure to proceed
- The safer alternative, reduced scope, or phased launch
- Who had decision authority
- Escalation used and why
- The resulting guardrail, review, or monitoring

Avoid claiming that legal, privacy, security, or safety partners were blockers. Show how you made their constraints actionable.

### Cost-quality trade-off

Cost and quality are not interchangeable scalar values. Discuss:

- Which quality failures mattered
- User or workload segments
- Latency and reliability effects
- Model routing, caching, context reduction, batching, distillation, or fallback options
- Marginal quality per unit cost
- Budget or capacity constraints
- Rollout and regression detection
- Cases that remained on a more expensive path

A strong story might show that you rejected a universal model downgrade and routed only lower-risk workloads.

### Failed launch

Separate launch failure from project failure. Cover:

- Launch hypothesis and gate
- What actually failed: model, data, UX, integration, operations, adoption, or positioning
- Early warning signals that were missed or discounted
- User or customer consequence
- Decision to pause, roll back, narrow, or continue
- Communication and accountability
- Corrective action
- What the next launch process changed

Do not redefine success after the fact.

### Data problems

Data stories should go beyond cleaning.

Cover:

- Provenance, consent, ownership, access, and retention
- Coverage and representativeness
- Label quality and ambiguity
- Leakage, skew, drift, and schema change
- Which downstream decisions were corrupted
- Whether the system failed closed, degraded, or continued
- Detection and contract changes
- Ownership of ongoing quality

For FDE stories, include the customer's system of record and responsibility boundaries. For Platform stories, include contracts and tenant isolation. For Product AI stories, include how the problem changed user behavior.

### Responsible AI

Responsible AI evidence is concrete risk management, not a values speech.

Explain:

- Intended use and foreseeable misuse
- Affected people and severity
- Data and model limitations
- Human oversight and appeal
- Transparency at the point of use
- Access controls and tool permissions
- Evaluation and red-team evidence
- Monitoring, incident handling, and retirement
- Who accepted residual risk

State what you did not know and who supplied expertise you did not have.

### Using AI coding tools responsibly

Interviewers may ask how you use coding agents, copilots, or model-generated analysis. Give a working method rather than claiming that you either trust or avoid them.

Cover:

- Tool policy and whether use was allowed
- Data classification and secrets handling
- Scope given to the tool
- How you inspected context and generated changes
- Tests, static checks, review, and runtime validation
- Verification of APIs, dependencies, licenses, and security assumptions
- Authorship and disclosure expectations
- Cases where you did not use the tool
- How you prevented automation bias

Strong evidence includes catching a plausible but incorrect generated change, correcting the validation process, and preserving human accountability for the result.

## Role-Specific Behavioral Signals

### Product AI Engineer

Interviewers need evidence that you connect model behavior to a user and product outcome.

Emphasize:

- Choosing a real user problem instead of forcing an AI feature
- Defining a product hypothesis and baseline
- Connecting evaluation slices to user experience
- Working across product, design, data, safety, privacy, and platform
- Shipping progressively with fallback and recovery
- Learning from failed interactions and feedback
- Balancing quality, latency, cost, safety, and usability
- Owning the full loop after launch

Useful stories:

- You stopped an attractive AI feature because the user problem was weak.
- You changed the UX because model uncertainty could not be eliminated.
- Offline evaluation improved while user outcomes did not.
- A model or retrieval change fixed one segment and harmed another.
- A launch gate or feedback loop changed future product decisions.

Weak signals:

- Discussing only prompts or model choice
- Claiming product success from an offline score
- Ignoring deterministic controls and failure recovery
- Treating safety and privacy as review steps after implementation
- Saying "the product team decided" without showing your input

See the [Product AI guide](04-product-ai-engineer.md) for the full role loop.

### Forward Deployed Engineer

Interviewers need evidence that you can protect customer trust while turning ambiguity into a supported production outcome.

Emphasize:

- Discovery that uncovers the real workflow and acceptance criteria
- Expectation setting and explicit non-goals
- Integration across customer data, identity, network, security, and operations
- Early communication of bad news
- Scope negotiation under deadline or commercial pressure
- Production ownership, handoff, and support boundaries
- Turning repeated field evidence into product improvements
- Explaining technical decisions to mixed audiences

Useful stories:

- You challenged a customer's requested solution while preserving the underlying goal.
- A proof of concept exposed a production blocker and you reset scope early.
- You handled an escalation before the root cause was known.
- You distinguished one-off customization from a reusable product gap.
- You negotiated ownership and support before launch.

Weak signals:

- Heroic customization with no maintenance plan
- Blaming the customer for unclear requirements or poor data
- Promising a date before validating dependencies
- Treating adoption as the account team's responsibility
- Hiding delivery risk to preserve short-term confidence

See the [FDE guide](05-forward-deployed-engineer.md) for discovery, delivery, and customer scenarios.

### ML/AI Platform Engineer

Interviewers need evidence that your infrastructure creates safe leverage for internal users.

Emphasize:

- Deciding what to centralize and what to leave local
- Internal customer discovery and platform product judgment
- Stable contracts, paved roads, and justified escape hatches
- Reliability, capacity, cost allocation, and incident ownership
- Multi-tenant security, privacy, governance, and auditability
- Migration, adoption, support load, and deprecation
- Balancing research flexibility with production operability
- Creating other owners instead of becoming the permanent bottleneck

Useful stories:

- You stopped a platform build because a library or standard was enough.
- You migrated teams from fragmented serving paths without blocking delivery.
- You resolved a capacity conflict between high-value workloads.
- You changed a platform contract after observing real developer behavior.
- An incident exposed an ownership or isolation gap and changed the operating model.

Weak signals:

- Measuring success only by utilization or infrastructure completion
- Mandating one path without discovery or escape hatches
- Ignoring migration and adoption
- Centralizing every concern
- Discussing reliability without user-visible impact

See the [ML/AI Platform guide](06-ml-ai-platform-engineer.md) for platform strategy and operations.

## Synthesized Behavioral Question Bank

Every question in this section is synthesized for practice. It is not attributed to a specific company and does not represent any company's values or interview process.

### Scope, ownership, and impact

1. Tell me about the largest outcome you personally owned. What made it difficult?
2. Describe a project that expanded beyond your original team. How did your role change?
3. Tell me about a result that would not have happened without your intervention.
4. Describe a time you inherited an important area with unclear ownership.
5. Tell me about a project where the visible output shipped but the intended outcome did not occur.
6. What durable change have you created that continued after you moved on?

### Ambiguity and prioritization

7. Tell me about a poorly defined AI problem that you turned into an executable plan.
8. Describe a time you stopped or substantially narrowed a promising project.
9. Tell me about a hard priority decision between two valuable initiatives.
10. Describe a decision you made before you had all the evidence you wanted.
11. Tell me about a time new evidence invalidated your roadmap.
12. How have you decided whether a repeated problem deserved a platform, product feature, or local solution?

### Influence, conflict, and communication

13. Tell me about a significant disagreement with a strong technical peer.
14. Describe a change you led when none of the required teams reported to you.
15. Tell me about a time you changed your mind because of another person's evidence.
16. Describe a decision that remained unpopular even after you made it.
17. Tell me about a time you communicated serious technical risk to a nontechnical decision-maker.
18. Describe a conflict caused by different incentives rather than different facts.

### Judgment, architecture, and strategy

19. Tell me about an architecture decision with long-term consequences.
20. Describe a technical strategy you shaped across multiple teams.
21. Tell me about a time the technically strongest design was not the right decision.
22. Describe a reversible decision you used to learn before committing.
23. Tell me about a standard, interface, or mechanism you created to improve decisions at scale.
24. Describe a case where centralization would have reduced duplication but still been the wrong choice.

### Failure, incidents, and accountability

25. Tell me about a failure caused in part by your own judgment.
26. Describe a production incident in which you made an important decision with incomplete information.
27. Tell me about a risk you identified too late.
28. Describe a time you had to deliver bad news before you knew the solution.
29. Tell me about a failed launch and how you decided what to do next.
30. Describe a post-incident change that altered behavior, not only documentation.

### AI quality, data, safety, and economics

31. Tell me about a time model quality was too uncertain for a simple launch decision.
32. Describe a disagreement over an AI evaluation and how it was resolved.
33. Tell me about a time you pushed back on an AI feature because of safety or privacy.
34. Describe a cost-quality trade-off that affected different users differently.
35. Tell me about a data problem that changed a product or architecture decision.
36. Describe how you used an AI coding tool on consequential work and verified the result.

### Customers, users, and platform adoption

37. Tell me about a customer escalation where trust was at risk.
38. Describe a time user behavior contradicted your technical assumptions.
39. Tell me about a platform capability that teams resisted adopting.
40. Describe a customer request you declined or reframed.
41. Tell me about a handoff or migration that failed to transfer ownership.
42. Describe how field or internal-user feedback changed a reusable product or platform capability.

### Mentoring and organizational capability

43. Tell me about someone you helped become an independent technical owner.
44. Describe a time your mentoring approach did not work.
45. Tell me about a team decision process you improved.
46. Describe how you raised the quality bar without becoming a review bottleneck.
47. Tell me about a time you delegated an important decision and accepted a different approach.
48. Describe an organizational weakness you addressed through people, process, or technology.

## High-Value Question Rubrics

The following 14 rubrics apply to synthesized questions from the bank. Score the observed evidence, not confidence or polish.

### Rubric 1: Largest owned outcome

Question: "Tell me about the largest outcome you personally owned. What made it difficult?"

Strong answer:

- Defines scope, stakes, duration, and personal ownership
- Names a decision rather than listing project tasks
- Explains cross-functional or technical complexity
- Separates individual action from team result
- Gives measured or observable impact
- Shows a limitation, trade-off, and lasting effect

Probing follow-ups:

- Which decision was yours alone?
- What did you delegate?
- Who disagreed with your approach?
- How was the result measured?
- What continued after you left?

Red flags:

- "We" throughout with no personal action
- Scale described only by team size or technology
- Impact is an output such as "we launched"
- No credible alternative
- Claims of staff scope without cross-team adoption or leverage

### Rubric 2: Poorly defined AI problem

Question: "Tell me about a poorly defined AI problem that you turned into an executable plan."

Strong answer:

- Begins with a genuinely unclear request
- Identifies users, workflow, baseline, constraints, and decision
- Prioritizes the unknown that could invalidate the work
- Uses discovery or a reversible experiment
- Sets acceptance criteria and non-goals
- Changes or stops the plan when evidence requires it

Probing follow-ups:

- What did the requester originally ask for?
- Which assumption was most dangerous?
- What did you refuse to decide yet?
- How did you know discovery was sufficient?
- What would have caused you to stop?

Red flags:

- Ambiguity means only missing implementation details
- Jumps immediately to a model or framework
- No user or baseline
- Endless discovery without a decision
- Retells a normal execution plan as ambiguity

### Rubric 3: Hard priority decision

Question: "Tell me about a hard priority decision between two valuable initiatives."

Strong answer:

- Makes both alternatives genuinely valuable
- Uses explicit criteria and constraints
- Includes opportunity cost and affected stakeholders
- Names the decision owner and decision process
- Communicates the decision to the losing side
- Reassesses against a stated signal

Probing follow-ups:

- Who lost resources because of your choice?
- Which criterion dominated?
- What did you get wrong?
- What signal would have reversed the ranking?
- Did you protect any option value?

Red flags:

- One option is obviously bad
- Uses urgency as the only criterion
- Hides behind leadership's decision
- No consequences for the deferred work
- Claims everything was eventually completed without trade-off

### Rubric 4: Significant disagreement

Question: "Tell me about a significant disagreement with a strong technical peer."

Strong answer:

- Represents the peer's position fairly
- Identifies different evidence, assumptions, or incentives
- Uses direct conversation and a decision mechanism
- Shows movement in both positions where appropriate
- Preserves the relationship
- Explains the final decision and result

Probing follow-ups:

- What was your peer right about?
- What did you say in the hardest conversation?
- Who had decision authority?
- How did you avoid false consensus?
- What happened to the relationship?

Red flags:

- The peer is portrayed as incompetent or political
- Candidate "wins" through status or escalation alone
- Conflict is merely a minor code-review comment
- No self-reflection
- Consensus is claimed without explaining the decision

### Rubric 5: Influence without authority

Question: "Describe a change you led when none of the required teams reported to you."

Strong answer:

- Maps stakeholders, incentives, and decision rights
- Earns credibility through evidence or useful work
- Tailors the path to different concerns
- Makes a real concession
- Creates a coalition or repeatable mechanism
- Demonstrates adoption beyond verbal agreement

Probing follow-ups:

- Why should the other teams care?
- Who resisted longest and why?
- What did you change in your proposal?
- What happened without executive escalation?
- Did adoption continue without you?

Red flags:

- Relies on a senior sponsor to order compliance
- Confuses presenting with influencing
- No competing priorities
- No adoption evidence
- Claims sole credit for collective alignment

### Rubric 6: Architecture decision

Question: "Tell me about an architecture decision with long-term consequences."

Strong answer:

- Defines the decision and credible alternatives
- Uses criteria tied to product, customer, reliability, security, cost, and operations
- Includes dissent and uncertainty
- States accepted risks
- Covers rollout, migration, observability, and rollback
- Revisits whether assumptions held

Probing follow-ups:

- Why was the rejected option credible?
- Which assumption had the weakest evidence?
- Who paid the migration cost?
- What failure mode worried you most?
- What would trigger a redesign?

Red flags:

- Technology preference presented as architecture judgment
- No migration or operational ownership
- Alternatives are straw men
- Uses current knowledge to judge an old decision
- Cannot state the decision's downside

### Rubric 7: Failure caused by judgment

Question: "Tell me about a failure caused in part by your own judgment."

Strong answer:

- Names a real miss and its consequence
- Explains what was knowable at the time
- Takes proportionate accountability
- Detects and communicates the failure
- Repairs immediate harm
- Changes a behavior or mechanism and shows later evidence

Probing follow-ups:

- What specifically was your mistake?
- Which warning did you discount?
- Who did you tell first?
- What consequence did the team or customer experience?
- How do you know the learning persisted?

Red flags:

- Disguised strength
- Blames process, leadership, or a junior teammate
- No consequence
- Learning is "communicate more"
- Claims the failure was unavoidable

### Rubric 8: Production incident

Question: "Describe a production incident in which you made an important decision with incomplete information."

Strong answer:

- Establishes user impact and role
- Prioritizes mitigation before complete diagnosis
- Uses clear incident coordination
- Explains a consequential decision and its risk
- Validates recovery
- Connects technical and organizational root causes
- Produces a concrete prevention or response change

Probing follow-ups:

- What did you deliberately not investigate first?
- Why was the mitigation safe?
- How did you communicate uncertainty?
- What nearly made the incident worse?
- Which follow-up had the highest leverage?

Red flags:

- Chronological debugging dump
- Hero narrative with no delegation
- Root cause equals one person's mistake
- No user impact or recovery validation
- Follow-up limited to "add monitoring"

### Rubric 9: Failed AI launch

Question: "Tell me about a failed launch and how you decided what to do next."

Strong answer:

- States the hypothesis and launch gate
- Identifies whether failure was model, data, UX, integration, operations, or adoption
- Acknowledges missed signals
- Protects users through pause, rollback, or reduced scope
- Avoids redefining success
- Changes the next launch or product decision

Probing follow-ups:

- What was the earliest missed warning?
- Who was affected?
- Why did you launch despite uncertainty?
- Who made the rollback decision?
- What evidence justified another attempt or cancellation?

Red flags:

- Calls any delay a failed launch
- Blames users for using the feature incorrectly
- Reports only model metrics
- Continues because of sunk cost
- No changed launch mechanism

### Rubric 10: Evaluation disagreement

Question: "Describe a disagreement over an AI evaluation and how it was resolved."

Strong answer:

- Locates disagreement in the objective, data, label, rubric, grader, threshold, or slice
- Includes domain and user perspectives
- Examines representative examples
- Handles ambiguous cases explicitly
- Establishes a decision rule and residual uncertainty
- Versions the evaluation as product behavior changes

Probing follow-ups:

- Who selected the examples?
- What did human reviewers disagree about?
- Which slice changed the decision?
- How did you test automated graders?
- What remained unresolved?

Red flags:

- Treats one benchmark as ground truth
- Uses majority vote without examining ambiguity
- Optimizes the metric without user evidence
- Cannot explain dataset provenance
- Dismisses domain experts as subjective

### Rubric 11: Safety or privacy pushback

Question: "Tell me about a time you pushed back on an AI feature because of safety or privacy."

Strong answer:

- Defines a concrete harm, exposure, or misuse path
- Separates evidence from uncertainty
- Understands data flow and affected users
- Offers a safer alternative or narrower launch
- Uses appropriate expertise and decision authority
- Shows the final guardrail and residual risk

Probing follow-ups:

- Who could have been harmed?
- What pressure favored launch?
- Why was your alternative sufficient?
- Who accepted residual risk?
- What monitoring detected new harm?

Red flags:

- Generic concern with no failure path
- Candidate claims sole ethical authority
- Legal or safety partners portrayed as obstacles
- No alternative or escalation path
- Confidential data entered into unapproved tools

### Rubric 12: Cost-quality trade-off

Question: "Describe a cost-quality trade-off that affected different users differently."

Strong answer:

- Defines relevant quality and cost measures
- Segments users or workloads by stakes
- Considers latency and reliability
- Evaluates routing, caching, batching, context, model, or fallback choices
- Protects high-risk cases
- Measures regressions after rollout

Probing follow-ups:

- Which quality failures were unacceptable?
- How did you estimate marginal value?
- Who paid the cost?
- Which segment stayed on the expensive path?
- What was your rollback threshold?

Red flags:

- Uses average quality only
- Assumes cheaper is automatically better
- No user segmentation
- Invented savings without a baseline
- Ignores provider or capacity failure modes

### Rubric 13: Customer escalation

Question: "Tell me about a customer escalation where trust was at risk."

Strong answer:

- Describes customer impact and stakes
- Establishes ownership boundaries
- Communicates before the root cause is known
- Separates immediate relief from durable correction
- Challenges assumptions without blaming the customer
- Restores trust through evidence and follow-through
- Feeds repeated learning into product or operations

Probing follow-ups:

- What did you say in the first update?
- Which commitment did you refuse to make?
- Was the requested fix actually the right fix?
- How did you coordinate account and engineering teams?
- What changed for future customers?

Red flags:

- Blames customer data or requirements
- Promises a date without evidence
- Solves with unsupported customization
- Hides uncertainty
- Treats relationship repair as someone else's job

### Rubric 14: Independent technical owner

Question: "Tell me about someone you helped become an independent technical owner."

Strong answer:

- Starts from the person's goals and a specific capability gap
- Gives actionable feedback and meaningful opportunity
- Adjusts support as evidence changes
- Transfers context and decision authority
- Avoids taking over
- Shows independent behavior, not only promotion or praise
- Reflects on the mentoring method

Probing follow-ups:

- What did the person want?
- What feedback was difficult to give?
- What did you let them do differently?
- When did you step back?
- What did you learn about your own approach?

Red flags:

- Takes credit for another person's promotion
- Mentoring means answering questions
- Gives opportunity without support or guardrails
- Makes the person copy the candidate's style
- Cannot name independent ownership afterward

## Common Red Flags Across Answers

### Evidence problems

- No specific event
- No personal decision or action
- Vague impact such as "it went well"
- Numbers with no source, baseline, or definition
- Confidential information shared unnecessarily
- A recent result presented without enough time to observe impact

### Judgment problems

- No credible alternative
- No downside to the chosen path
- Hindsight presented as obvious foresight
- Irreversible commitment before learning
- One metric treated as the complete decision
- Risk transferred to users, customers, or another team without acknowledgment

### Leadership problems

- Influence means escalating to a powerful sponsor
- Conflict answer makes the other person look foolish
- Accountability becomes personal blame
- Delegation means loss of visibility
- Mentoring creates dependence
- Staff claims rely on meeting attendance rather than organizational change

### AI-specific problems

- Model quality discussed as one aggregate score
- Offline evaluation treated as product success
- Data provenance, privacy, or authorization omitted
- Safety added after launch
- Cost reduced without checking quality by segment
- AI-generated code accepted because tests passed
- Human review invoked without explaining reviewer expertise, disagreement, or operating cost

### Communication problems

- More than half the answer is context
- Chronology replaces decision-making
- Acronyms or company-specific names make the story hard to follow
- The answer never stops
- Learning is generic and unsupported
- Follow-up answers contradict the opening

## Handle Probing Follow-Ups

Interviewers probe to distinguish rehearsed summaries from lived experience. Prepare direct answers to:

- What exactly did you do?
- What did someone else do?
- Who had decision authority?
- What was the hardest trade-off?
- What did you know at the time?
- Which assumption was wrong?
- What metric did you use and why?
- Where did that data come from?
- Who disagreed?
- What did they say?
- What did you change because of their argument?
- What did you communicate to leadership or the customer?
- What happened after launch?
- What would you do differently?
- What would a staff engineer have done differently?

Use a three-part follow-up response:

1. Answer the question in the first sentence.
2. Give the evidence or example.
3. State the consequence.

If you do not know or remember, say so precisely. Do not fill the gap with a plausible reconstruction.

## Reuse Stories Deliberately

Story reuse is normal. Repetition without additional evidence is the problem.

Use these rules:

1. Maintain at least eight interview-ready stories, with all 12 slots drafted.
2. Use no single story as the primary answer more than twice across one interview loop.
3. Do not reuse a story with the same interviewer unless they ask to return to it.
4. Reuse only when the question exposes a genuinely different decision or competency.
5. Keep facts, dates, scope, and metrics consistent across versions.
6. Do not recast the same action as sole ownership in one round and team ownership in another.
7. Prefer a smaller distinct story over a famous story that does not answer the question.
8. Reserve one failure, one conflict, and one safety or ethical story. Do not make every answer a success story.
9. Track which stories the recruiter says may be discussed by multiple interviewers.
10. Tell an interviewer when the event has already been covered, then offer either a new example or a different angle.

Acceptable reuse:

- Architecture question - focus on options, criteria, and migration.
- Influence question using the same event - focus on incentives, dissent, and adoption.

Weak reuse:

- Repeating the entire project narrative and changing only the final sentence.

## Interviewer Calibration

Behavioral interviews are vulnerable to inconsistent standards. Whether you are preparing as a candidate or running mocks, calibrate the evidence before scoring.

### Before the interview

Define:

- Target level
- Competencies assigned to the round
- Observable positive signals
- Disqualifying or high-risk signals
- Expected scope for the role
- Whether role-specific depth is required
- Which questions are primary and which are probes

Do not define staff as "better senior." State the additional organizational and leverage evidence expected.

### During the interview

Interviewers should:

- Ask the same core competency question across candidates when practical
- Probe ownership, decisions, alternatives, impact, and learning
- Separate communication polish from evidence quality
- Avoid rewarding stories merely because the domain is familiar
- Avoid penalizing a candidate for confidential details they cannot share
- Record observed evidence before assigning a score
- Distinguish "not demonstrated" from negative evidence

Candidate strategy:

- Ask what level and competency the round evaluates
- Clarify whether the interviewer wants one deep example or several shorter ones
- State the role and scope before technical detail
- Invite a follow-up after the 90-second version

### After the interview

Score each competency independently. Do not let one impressive project determine every category.

Separate:

- Evidence - what the candidate said they did
- Inference - what the interviewer believes it indicates
- Gap - what was not established
- Risk - what could matter in the role

Avoid inventing a cultural standard that was not defined before the interview.

## Measurable Behavioral Scorecard

Use the canonical scale from [module 09](09-mock-interviews-and-scorecards.md#common-scoring-scale). Score each dimension from 1 to 4:

- 1 - Weak or unsafe: missing, contradicted, materially below scope, or usable only after direct rescue
- 2 - Partial: relevant evidence appears, but it is incomplete, generic, or dependent on prompting
- 3 - Interview-ready: clear, credible, independent evidence appropriate for the target level
- 4 - Strong senior or staff evidence: unusually specific, adaptive evidence with depth, trade-offs, and durable impact

### Core dimensions

1. Scope and stakes
   - Is the affected boundary clear?
   - Does it match senior or staff expectations?
2. Personal ownership
   - Are decisions and individual actions explicit?
   - Are team contributions represented accurately?
3. Ambiguity handling
   - Did the candidate identify unknowns, assumptions, and the next learning step?
4. Judgment and trade-offs
   - Were credible alternatives compared?
   - Was accepted risk explicit?
5. Influence and collaboration
   - Were incentives, disagreement, and decision rights handled well?
6. Execution and accountability
   - Did the candidate own commitments, communication, and consequences?
7. Impact and measurement
   - Is the result observable and honestly measured?
8. Learning and adaptation
   - Did behavior or a mechanism change?
9. AI production judgment
   - Where relevant, did the answer cover evaluation, data, safety, privacy, latency, cost, and operations?
10. Communication
   - Was the answer direct, structured, concise, and resilient to probes?

The minimum is 10 and the maximum is 40.

### Interpret the score

- 10-19 - Weak profile. Core evidence is missing, unsafe, or not defensible.
- 20-27 - Developing profile. Some stories work, but important competencies require prompts or stronger examples.
- 28-34 - Practice-ready profile. Much of the evidence is credible; repair every dimension below 3.
- 35-40 - Strong profile. Confirm that every required dimension meets the per-dimension and repeated-mock rules before declaring readiness.

These ranges are practice heuristics, not hiring predictions or readiness labels. A total alone cannot confer readiness, and a high total does not erase a 1 in ownership, judgment, integrity, or accountability.

### Add a level check

After the numeric score, record one:

- Below senior evidence
- Senior evidence
- Mixed senior and staff evidence
- Staff evidence
- Insufficient evidence to level

For a staff rating, require clear evidence in all four areas:

- Cross-team or organizational scope
- Influence across different incentives
- Durable leverage or a reusable mechanism
- Strategy, sequencing, adoption, or governance beyond direct execution

## Run a 45-Minute Mock

Use the [mock interview and scorecard guide](09-mock-interviews-and-scorecards.md) for the broader simulation program.

### Interviewer setup

Choose:

- One target role
- One target level
- Three competencies
- Three primary synthesized questions
- Two probes for each
- One role-specific AI topic

Do not give the candidate the exact questions in advance.

### Mock schedule

1. Minutes 0-3 - introduction, target level, and format
2. Minutes 3-13 - first question and probes
3. Minutes 13-23 - second question and probes
4. Minutes 23-33 - third question and probes
5. Minutes 33-38 - role-specific AI scenario
6. Minutes 38-42 - candidate questions
7. Minutes 42-45 - silent scoring

Give feedback after scoring so the discussion does not rewrite the evidence in memory.

### Feedback format

The interviewer provides:

- Strongest observed evidence
- Most important missing evidence
- One sentence that was unclear or too vague
- One probe the answer could not withstand
- One level-calibration judgment
- One drill before the next mock

The candidate records:

- Story used
- Actual duration
- Score by dimension
- Factual or consistency issue
- Follow-up that caused difficulty
- Exact revision to make
- Date for the next retrieval attempt

## Questions to Ask Interviewers

Choose questions that help you understand real scope, constraints, and decision quality. Do not ask all of them.

### Level and success

- What outcomes distinguish a strong senior engineer from a strong staff engineer on this team?
- Which decisions would this role own in the first six months?
- What is an example of impact at the expected level?
- Where has the team most needed technical leadership during the last year?
- How do you evaluate influence when an engineer has no formal authority?

### Product AI

- How does the team connect offline evaluation to user and business outcomes?
- Who decides when model quality is sufficient to launch?
- How are product, engineering, design, safety, privacy, and platform responsibilities divided?
- What failure modes currently limit the product most?
- How does the team decide when deterministic behavior should replace or constrain model behavior?

### Forward Deployed Engineering

- Which stages does the FDE own from discovery through production support?
- How are customer commitments validated before dates or scope are promised?
- What separates reusable product work from customer-specific implementation?
- Who owns production incidents and long-term maintenance after handoff?
- How does field evidence influence product and platform priorities?

### ML/AI Platform

- Who are the platform's primary internal users, and how is adoption measured?
- Which capabilities are intentionally centralized, and which remain team-owned?
- What are the most important reliability, capacity, or cost constraints today?
- How do teams request an escape hatch from the paved road?
- What migration or deprecation has been hardest, and why?

### Culture through behavior

- Tell me about a recent technical disagreement and how the team resolved it.
- What happens when a launch gate conflicts with a customer or revenue deadline?
- How does the team respond when an evaluation result is ambiguous?
- What is an example of a decision the team reversed after new evidence?
- How are incident learning and accountability handled?

The answer matters more than the label. Ask for examples when the response remains abstract.

## Readiness Gate

[Module 09 readiness rules](09-mock-interviews-and-scorecards.md#readiness-rules) are canonical. The requirements below add behavioral preparation and role-specific evidence; they do not replace or weaken the canonical rules. Complete the gate without notes.

### Story-bank coverage

- [ ] I have all 12 story slots drafted.
- [ ] At least eight stories are distinct events.
- [ ] I have one real failure, conflict, incident, and ethical or safety decision.
- [ ] I have two stories with role-specific AI depth.
- [ ] I have at least two stories that can support staff-level evidence if I am targeting staff.
- [ ] Every story identifies my actions and other people's contributions accurately.

### Evidence quality

- [ ] Every metric has a known source, definition, baseline, and time window, or is labeled as an estimate.
- [ ] I can explain impact without relying on a fabricated number.
- [ ] Every primary story includes a credible alternative and trade-off.
- [ ] Every primary story includes a concrete learning or changed mechanism.
- [ ] I can state what I did not know.
- [ ] I have removed or generalized confidential details.

### Delivery

- [ ] I can deliver each primary story in 30, 90, and 180 seconds.
- [ ] The action section occupies most of the 90-second version.
- [ ] I can stop after the opening and answer probes directly.
- [ ] A listener can repeat my decision, action, and impact.
- [ ] I do not use one story more than twice in a mock loop.
- [ ] My facts remain consistent across versions.

### Level and role

- [ ] My examples match the expected scope instead of relying on title.
- [ ] I can explain why a story demonstrates senior, staff, or neither.
- [ ] Product AI stories connect model behavior to users and product outcomes.
- [ ] FDE stories connect customer trust to production delivery and reusable learning.
- [ ] Platform stories connect infrastructure to internal-user leverage, adoption, and operations.
- [ ] Staff stories show cross-team influence, durable leverage, and organizational effect.

### Mock performance

- [ ] My three most recent full behavioral mocks used different prompts.
- [ ] At least two mocks were scored by different interviewers, or one was independently double-scored.
- [ ] Every required dimension scored at least 3 in at least two of the three mocks.
- [ ] No required dimension scored 1 in either of the two most recent mocks.
- [ ] I finished the core scope within time in at least two mocks.
- [ ] I handled at least one material follow-up without direct rescue in each of the two most recent mocks.
- [ ] Every known repeated failure completed the focused and full re-tests required by module 09.
- [ ] No unresolved hard fail remains.
- [ ] No ownership, judgment, integrity, or accountability red flag remains unresolved.
- [ ] I repeated the weakest story after feedback on a fresh prompt and demonstrated the target behavior.
- [ ] I have five questions ready for the actual interviewers.

Passing the gate means repeated mock evidence supports readiness for this format. No score band or single total can override the per-dimension, timing, follow-up, independence, hard-fail, and re-test requirements. It does not predict a hiring decision. Company-specific competencies, interviewer calibration, and role scope still matter.

## Seven-Day Practice Loop

### Day 1: Inventory

- List 20 possible events from the last several years.
- Map them to the 12 story slots.
- Reject events where your agency or consequence is too weak.

### Day 2: Evidence

- Complete SAIL for the strongest eight events.
- Verify metrics, dates, scope, and contributions.
- Mark unknown or confidential details.

### Day 3: Decisions

- Write the core decision, alternatives, trade-off, and accepted risk for each story.
- Replace generic action verbs with observable actions.

### Day 4: Delivery

- Record 180-second and 90-second versions.
- Remove context that does not explain the decision.
- Create 30-second indexes.

### Day 5: Probes

- Answer the follow-up defense prompts without notes.
- Practice admitting what you do not know.
- Check consistency across reused stories.

### Day 6: Mock

- Run the 45-minute format.
- Score before discussing.
- Choose one weak dimension and one weak story.

### Day 7: Repair

- Rewrite only the weak section.
- Repeat the failed question with a different opening.
- Schedule the next mock with different competencies.
